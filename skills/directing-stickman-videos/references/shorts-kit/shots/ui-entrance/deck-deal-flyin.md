## Intent
Give suspense first (what is this stack?), then the answer (dozens of items that fly into the page and take their places by themselves). The viewer should feel "things pouring in without end", with each card slamming into place with urgency.

## Motion core
- First half (pile-orbit-open, optional): all cards are stacked into a physical deck with real height, sitting on a dark brushed-graphite tabletop beside the page board. The top light hits only the deck, and the camera orbits the pile in a side-angled close-up. While pulling back, the "light" lights the dark page board with a soft-edged aperture from the deck. The page board is always a display board on a dark table, and the dark field around it runs through to the last frame
- **Anticipation beat (borrowed from Disney principle 2)**: before the first card is dealt, give one section-level beat of anticipation. The deck presses down slightly as a whole + the top card pulls back opposite to the deal direction, so the viewer's eyes are led to the deck before watching the deal. Anticipation is done once at the section level, **not added per card** (per-card anticipation would drag down the hard-acceleration beat of R2)
- Second half (deck-deal): 26 cards are dealt from the deck to the grid targets in reading order, and the gap between deals shrinks with hard acceleration. Each card's flight has a z arc apex, an overshoot settle and a landing press rebound (settle/press is the follow-through of principle 5: a fast stop comes with a cushion, and cutting it reads as a hard freeze)
- The camera follows the deal toward the end of the board (faster and faster) while rising, tilting back and turning sideways, and lands on a runway-style overhead: the whole board stretches diagonally into the distance, all 26 cards are in frame, and the far end is darkened for depth of field. After the board is full it holds still (> 0.5s, only 1.5% very slow push-in), and a tagline rises at the bottom left with a mono counter line (the counter jumps with the number of landed cards). During the closing pause, layers settle on different frames: card bodies settle first, shadow convergence lags about 3f, and the ghost trails fade last (drag hierarchy, borrowed from principle 5; freezing every element on the same frame reads as mechanical)

The 0.5s rest stillness precedent is unchanged: the layer offset happens at the head of the rest section and everything is locked by the end of rest (the pattern has been extracted to assets/lib/helpers/motion.ts lagged) |
| Motion blur (single card) | during flight a 5% path-lag blur(6px) ghost, opacity 0.25·(1−t) | A ghost trail is cheaper than true motion blur and good enough; keep this approach at the single-card level |

## Sound
During the orbit pull-back + first batch of deals, pin whoosh-big (the template film pins it at f308), and during the deal acceleration pin whoosh-fast (f340/356). Three ways to avoid a machine-gun feel in the landing sounds of a rapid sequence: alternate two samples, step the volume down, and let the spacing follow the acceleration curve of the animation. When it gets so dense it smears, fade the sound out into one swoosh and do not score card by card (S2).

## Known traps
- An abstract flood style did not converge over many rounds; switching to the physical metaphor of "dealing cards" passed in one round (R2/B3). For group motion, find the physical metaphor first and then write code
- Card textures must be slices of real page elements (Q1); hand-made fake cards look fake at a glance. Extras reuse real card slices placed with an offset
- All flying at once reads as an explosion; the staggered, ever-faster rhythm is the soul. If the first-half orbit is cut, the deal start needs another explanation of "where the deck came from"
- The anticipation/follow-through techniques are borrowed from the Disney 12 principles (the distilled version built into the pixel2motion skill). The anticipation amplitude has a user precedent (2026-07-09: "OK, I can see it now", after two small amplitudes before that were both imperceptible). The drag-hierarchy frame offset is still the default advice; when it conflicts with the R2 precedent (for example the user asks for faster and harder), the precedent wins
- The textbook ratio (anticipation = 20–30% of the main action) yields too small a pixel displacement at a pulled-back camera: **compute pixels first, then ratio**, because promo viewers watch the wide shot, not the close-up
- `<Sequence>` rebases the frame number, and the internal Freeze of CameraMotionBlur makes PageCam sample the wrong frame (a horizontal mis-blur breaks the shot). For shots choreographed in absolute frames, pass PageCam a `frame` prop to restore the absolute frame (the lib component already supports this)
